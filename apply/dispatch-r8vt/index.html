Q1How do you thrive in a startup environment? How do you need to adapt as an EM to be effective at a startup?

I do my best work when the distance between the team and the customer is short. Most of my career has been at early-stage companies. At EarlyDay I was the founding CTO and first employee. I wrote the first version of the platform, hired the whole team, and kept every engineer through our acquisition by Wonderschool. I also helped raise $4M and ran technical due diligence. If something needed doing, from hiring to AWS setup to compliance checklists, I did it.

The biggest adjustment for an EM at a startup is that you can't manage from a distance. I stay close to code reviews, design discussions and customers, so my judgement on estimates and trade-offs is informed. My job is to hire and grow great people, and to be the link between Product, engineering and the business so we work on the right thing at the right time.

Process should be just enough to ship quickly with quality and confidence. I add it when we feel the pain and remove it when it stops paying off.

Q2Describe your most effective relationship with the team from a technical perspective. What role do you play? What are you accountable for and what is the team accountable for?

At Spring Health I lead 15 engineers across three teams in the US and Latin America. Together we own about 15,000 telehealth sessions a day across Spring Health and Alma, plus clinical notes and compliance.

A good example is our move from a legacy video vendor to a custom platform on Zoom's Video SDK. We couldn't drop sessions, and we had mobile apps, external clients, and separate provider and member experiences. I made the business case to leadership, planned resourcing, and owned the timeline and KPIs. The engineers owned the technical plan, the trade-offs, their estimates and the quality of what shipped. Session failures fell from about 15% to under 5%, under budget.

I see my technical role as architecture partner, advocate for technical excellence, and unblocker. Staff engineers and tech leads own technical decisions; I make sure they're made deliberately, with failure modes and business outcomes considered. I'm accountable for direction, quality, delivery and people. The team owns design, estimates, and what they ship to production. As a team matures, I step back from the code and spend more time removing friction and protecting time for technical work.

Q3What strategies, processes, and methodologies do you operationalize to get your teams to execute most effectively?

My principle is the lightest process that keeps the team shipping with confidence, and none of it is permanent.

Wonderschool is my clearest example. The team shipped rarely and carefully, with high support volume, legacy code and low accountability. We rebuilt the delivery system in layers. We instrumented the platform with Datadog and worked through the alert backlog instead of ignoring it. We moved CI/CD to GitHub Actions so anything merged to main ships to production, which made ownership clear. We shipped larger changes dark behind feature flags. And we fixed flaky tests, added coverage checks, migrated JavaScript to TypeScript and added end-to-end tests. Support tickets fell 85%, and the team went from scared to release to shipping often.

Day to day, that means clear sprint outcomes agreed with Product, small shippable tickets, design docs for larger bets, blameless post-mortems with follow-ups that actually get done, and protected time for staff engineers to tackle the technical debt that slows everyone down.

Q4Describe your experience with the end-to-end process of hiring and onboarding engineers. Where do you have experience being hands-on throughout this process?

I've hired dozens of engineers, as an interviewer and as the hiring manager, and I've been hands-on at every stage: headcount and budget planning, job descriptions, designing the process, running loops, reference calls, offers and closing.

At EarlyDay there was no recruiter, so I hired every engineer and designer myself. At Wonderschool I hired about eight engineers and rebuilt the loop. Strong coders were getting through without systems thinking, so we added system design, pair programming and a leadership interview, and trained interviewers so the bar stayed consistent. I also defined engineering levels for the company. At Spring Health I've hired seven engineers, with 100% retention on my teams. Remotely published a case study on that model, and three other managers at Spring Health now use it.

Onboarding matters as much as hiring. Where possible, a new engineer ships a small change to production on day one, then works to a 30-60-90 day plan with regular check-ins. I ask every new hire for feedback so the next person has a better experience.

Q5Describe your philosophy around, and experience with, AI technologies and tools. How have you personally leveraged these tools and what have you built? How have your teams used them?

AI is leverage, not magic. It makes good engineers faster, and it's only as trustworthy as your ability to measure it.

I've shipped AI in production since GPT-3. At EarlyDay we replaced manual résumé data entry by contractors with LLM extraction that was more reliable, and teacher approvals went from 48 business hours to minutes. At Spring Health we use session transcripts with LLMs to power in-product experiences, with a strong focus on safety and HIPAA.

Personally, I build with AI coding agents every day: more than 2,500 commits this year across a car rental platform with a booking voice agent, an AI concierge for home service businesses, and a volunteer platform for a Montana nonprofit. I've also built a personal finance app on Plaid and MX, and The Bot Portfolio, an agent that trades a real brokerage account with guardrails in code and a public audit log.

With teams, I focus on curiosity. Tools and models change every week, and some senior engineers were put off by early experiences. I encourage people to try new tools, share what works, and measure the impact, as we did when we adopted AI code review early at Wonderschool.

If I join

How I'd start

No grand plan before I've met the team. This is how I start everywhere.

Ship something and join on-call

The same thing I ask of every engineer I hire: a small change in production in the first week. It's the quickest way to learn the codebase, the deploy path and where it hurts.

Learn where the problems really are

One-to-ones with every engineer, time with Rafi and Design, and sitting in on customer calls, so I understand where NIGOs, failed syncs and slow openings actually come from before changing anything.

Change only what's earned

Agree a few metrics with Product and the team, then fix what's clearly costing us, the way we did at Wonderschool. Hiring starts as soon as we know which gaps matter most.
